# Week 3 — The Genre

## Genre is a contract

You've got your premise. Now, before we build anything, we name the genre. Genre is not the label they slap on the spine so the store knows which shelf to use. Genre is a contract.

Someone picks up a thriller. They've been promised tension that keeps ratcheting, and a danger that costs the hero something if they fail. Someone picks up a romance. They've been promised two people and an ending that pays off everything built between them. A literary novel promises depth, and prose worth slowing down for. The reader knows all this before they read a single word. That promise is why they picked up the book.

So genre decides, early: what the reader expects to feel, roughly how long the book runs, how fast it moves, and which choices land versus feel like a betrayal. Build the whole book blind and you break your own contract without meaning to. A mystery with no fair solution. A romance that ends cold. A thriller that never tightens. The reader won't name what went wrong. They'll feel cheated and put the book down.

## Name your promise

Every genre makes one core promise. Thriller: the walls close in. Mystery: here's a puzzle, played fair, that you could solve if you were sharp enough. Romance: this relationship will earn its ending. Fantasy: this world has rules, and they'll hold. Literary: I'll show you a life or a question, honestly. Science fiction: I'll take one idea and follow it to what it costs real people.

Find the one at the center of your book and commit to keeping it. Everything else you can bend. This you can't. Break it and you lose the reader, and no beautiful writing buys them back. Write your promise in one sentence. If you can't, you don't yet know what your reader came for.

## Convention versus cliché

A convention is a required part of the genre. A mystery needs a crime and an investigation. A romance needs two people held apart by something real. A fantasy needs a system the world runs on. Leave them out and you're not in the genre anymore.

A cliché is the tired, worn-out way people execute a convention. The detective who drinks too much and lost his partner. The farm boy who turns out to be chosen. The couple split by a misunderstanding one honest conversation would fix. A cliché is the first version that pops into your head, because it's the one you've seen a hundred times.

Deliver the convention, ditch the cliché. Give the reader the satisfaction they came for, through a door they didn't see coming. Your mystery still has a detective. It just isn't the detective from every other mystery. Keep the promise, refresh the delivery. You can only dodge a cliché if you can name it, which is why you read your genre.

## Read your genre

You can't write well in a genre you don't read. It's the floor. Reading your genre shows you the conventions in play now, what's been run into the ground, what readers expect today, and where the open ground is. The writer who doesn't read current thrillers writes the thriller from twenty years ago and doesn't know it. Read the classics for the foundation. Read recent bestsellers for the present. If you're writing in a genre you don't read, that's a signal: wrong genre, or reading to do before you build.

## When your book blends genres

Most books lean on more than one genre. That's fine, and often what makes a book its own thing. But pick a primary. The primary is the genre whose promise your reader most needs kept, and the shelf you most belong on. It sets your main contract, length, and pace. The other is what you borrow from to make the first richer. One is the spine, one is the thread. Try to fully deliver two primary contracts at once and you land neither.

## Task

Name your genre, list its promises, and mark which conventions you'll honor and which clichés you'll subvert. Then ask honestly whether you read enough in this genre to write it well. If not, name the books you need to read first, and read them. That's the difference between writing your genre and guessing at it.
