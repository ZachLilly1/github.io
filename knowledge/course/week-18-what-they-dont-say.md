# Week 18 — What They Don't Say

## Dialogue does two jobs at once

Good dialogue always does more than one thing: reveals character, advances plot, builds tension, delivers information, ideally several at once. Dialogue that only conveys information is dead. Real dialogue is a negotiation where two people want different things and the words are their tactics.

## Subtext is the whole game

People rarely say exactly what they mean. Dialogue with no gap between what's said and what's meant is flat. Subtext is the meaning underneath the words, two people argue about the dishes but are really fighting about respect. Let characters evade, deflect, imply, and lie. Trust the reader to read between the lines. On-the-nose dialogue, where characters state their exact feelings and intentions, is the surest mark of an amateur. The heat is in the gap between what they say and what they want.

## Real, but not actually real

Real speech is full of ums, repetitions, and boring pleasantries. Fictional dialogue is a distillation that sounds real while being tighter and sharper. Cut the greetings and the filler. Enter conversations late and leave early. Give each character a distinct verbal signature, vocabulary, rhythm, sentence length, so the reader can tell who's speaking without tags. Read it aloud to test whether it sounds like a human or like an author.

## Mechanics

Use "said" as your default attribution, it's invisible and readers skim past it. Avoid the said-bookisms, "exclaimed," "retorted," "expostulated," and adverb-laden tags like "she said angrily." Show the anger instead. Use action beats to attribute and characterize at once: "He set down his glass. 'I'm leaving.'" Give each speaker their own paragraph. Deploy silence, interruption, and what a character refuses to answer as tools.

## Task

Write a scene carried entirely by dialogue, with the real meaning under the surface. No character states their true feeling outright. Give each speaker a distinct voice, and let silence and evasion do as much work as the words.
