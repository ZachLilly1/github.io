# Week 24 — The Finish

## Openings and endings

The opening has to do several jobs fast: establish voice, ground the reader in a character and situation, raise a question that pulls them forward, and promise the kind of book this is. You don't need a gimmick, you need tension, something unresolved. Start as late into the story as possible, at the moment change becomes inevitable, not with backstory or a character waking up. The first page sells the second. The first chapter sells the book. Avoid the tired openings, weather alone, a dream, a backstory dump, a character alone thinking before anything has started.

The ending has to deliver on every promise the book made: resolve the central conflict, complete the arc, and answer the thematic question, or answer it by refusing to. A satisfying ending is surprising and inevitable at once, not what the reader predicted, but what, in hindsight, had to happen. The climax is the point of maximum pressure where the protagonist makes the decisive, character-defining choice, using what they've learned. They resolve their own story. Luck or a minor character saving them robs the ending. Then the resolution is brief: show the new normal, confirm the change, release the tension, and get out. Overlong endings deflate. Stop soon after the climax lands.

## Knowing it's done

A novel is never perfect, only finished. At some point more tinkering just moves things sideways instead of forward. When the structure holds, the prose is clean, and readers aren't finding new problems, it's done. Ship it. The unfinished perfect novel helps no one, and the finished imperfect one is the whole point.

## The path out

Format to industry standard: double-spaced, twelve-point serif, one-inch margins, indented paragraphs, a header with name and title. Then choose the path. Traditional, query agents with a polished manuscript, a one-page query letter, and often a synopsis, trading time and control for advances and distribution. Or self-publish, keeping control and higher royalties but owning every cost and responsibility. Neither is superior. Both demand the manuscript be as good as you can make it first, because no path fixes a weak book. Most first novels teach you to write novels. Finish, learn, and write the next one better.

## Task

Finalize your opening pages so they establish voice, ground the reader, and raise a question, starting as late as possible. Confirm the ending pays off the arc and the central question, with the protagonist resolving their own story. Then build your submission or publishing plan.
