# Week 16 — Voice on the Page

## Free indirect style

This is the most powerful tool in third-person fiction, and the one that separates flat prose from prose that lives. Free indirect style lets the narration slide into the character's own idiom without quotation marks and without "he thought." Instead of "She looked at the cake and thought it was disgusting," you write "The cake sat there, sweating, obscene." The judgment is the character's, delivered in the narrator's voice. It collapses the distance between reader and character, and it's worth mastering above almost any other technique.

## How it works

You take on the character's diction, their rhythm, their way of seeing, inside third-person narration. The narration stops being a neutral camera and starts being colored by the mind it's following. A bitter character's narration turns bitter. A frightened character's narration goes short and clipped. You're not describing the character's state from outside. You're letting their state saturate the prose itself. Done well, the reader stops noticing the narrator at all and simply lives inside the character.

## Filtering kills it

The enemy of close voice is filtering, those little phrases that put a pane of glass between reader and character. "She saw the door open." "He felt afraid." "She noticed the smell." Cut the filter and go direct. "The door opened." "Fear closed his throat." "The smell hit her." Every "she saw," "he felt," "she noticed" is a place where you're reporting the experience instead of delivering it. Strip them out and the prose snaps into immediacy.

## Task

Write a scene fully in your protagonist's voice using free indirect style. Let their diction and rhythm color the narration itself, not just their dialogue. Then hunt every filtering phrase, "saw," "felt," "noticed," "watched," and cut it so the experience lands direct.
