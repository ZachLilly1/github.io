# Week 20 — The World, the Question, and the Draft

## Setting as a living force

Setting is not backdrop. It's an active force that shapes mood, constrains action, and reflects theme, and it should be rendered through the POV character's perception, colored by their emotional state, so description doubles as characterization. Ground every scene in physical space early, or the reader floats. But avoid the real-estate-listing dump. Deliver setting in motion, woven through action and a few sharp sensory details, and engage more than sight, sound, smell, texture, temperature. One vivid, specific detail grounds a scene faster than a catalog.

## The question underneath

Theme is what the book is about beneath the plot, the central question it explores. Not a moral you impose, but a genuine question you investigate through the story: what does loyalty cost, can people change, is justice possible. The best themes are questions the author doesn't fully know the answer to. And theme works best when it emerges from the want-and-need arc rather than being announced. Write toward a question, not a message, and let the events be your exploration of it. Readers resent a lecture and embrace being made to feel and think.

## The draft only has to exist

Now you write. The cardinal rule of drafting: give yourself permission to write badly. The first draft's only job is to exist, to get the whole shape down so you have something to revise. Perfectionism is the enemy of completion. You can't edit a blank page. Silence the internal critic while drafting and turn it on during revision. A finished bad draft is worth infinitely more than a perfect first chapter and nothing after it. Momentum beats quality here. Set a sustainable daily count, build a routine, and protect it.

## Task

Ground a scene in a living setting, filtered through your POV character's emotion. Name the question your novel is really asking. Then set your drafting schedule and daily word count, and start the draft in earnest.
