# Week 4 — Lock It Down

## Why we stop and lock

Three weeks gathered pieces: a premise, a genre and its promise, the stakes underneath. This week we don't add anything new. We assemble. Everything from here on gets built on top of the foundation. If the foundation shifts while you build, the whole thing cracks. You'll be forty scenes deep and realize your stakes changed or your genre drifted, and half of what you wrote points at the wrong target. So before we build the people, we lock the ground they stand on. One page, pressure-tested until it holds. Once locked, it doesn't move.

## Assemble the sheet

Pull up the concept sheet and fill it in, not by copying old notes but by looking at each piece again now that it's settled. Read the premise line from Week 2. Does it still hold, or have three weeks sharpened it? Rewrite if needed. This is your last clean chance before the build. Then genre and promise from Week 3. Check them against each other, because here's where foundations crack: premise and genre have to agree. A quiet character study named as a thriller means one of them is lying. Fix the mismatch now, on the page, not forty scenes later. Then the protagonist in a line or two. Enough to anchor the rest.

## Want and need, side by side

The two lines that drive the whole book. The want is the external goal, the concrete thing the character chases, what they think will fix their life. The need is the internal thing they must accept or become to be whole. The rule to burn in: want and need pull against each other. The character chases the want, and the pursuit forces them to confront the need, usually the opposite of what they thought they wanted. Write them both, one above the other, and look at the gap. That gap is your book. No gap means the character has nowhere to go. Push on the need until it contradicts the want.

## Stakes, external and internal

What does failure cost. External stakes: what the character loses in the world. Internal stakes: what they lose in themselves. Weak books only have external stakes, so we watch without leaning in. Strong books put both on the table, so failure would cost the character the world and the self. Fill both lines, and make them cost something real. If the stakes feel safe, raise them until they don't.

## Pressure-test the whole thing

Read the full sheet top to bottom as one object. Does the premise hold? Do genre and premise agree? Does the want pull against the need? Do the stakes cost something real, inside and out? Is there a clear reason this book has to exist? Every answer yes means the foundation is locked and you're ready to build people on it. Any answer no is the sheet doing its job. Better to find the crack now, on one page, than after a hundred pages sit on top of it. Fix it, then lock it.

## Task

Finalize the one-page concept sheet. Fill every field: working title, premise line, genre, promise, protagonist, want, need, central obstacle, external and internal stakes, and why this book. Leave nothing blank. A blank field is a hole in the foundation. Run the pressure-test questions. When every answer is yes, the foundation is locked and doesn't change from here.
