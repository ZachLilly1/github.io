# Week 11 — Other Frameworks

## Three-act is not the only shape

You've learned three-act because it's the most reliable, but it isn't the only structure, and forcing every story into it is a mistake. This week you learn the alternatives so you can choose the shape that fits your book instead of bending your book to fit a shape.

## The options

The seven-point structure offers more granular beats: hook, first plot turn, first pinch, midpoint, second pinch, second plot turn, resolution. It's three-act with more handrails, useful if you want tighter guidance through the middle.

Freytag's pyramid, exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, denouement, suits tragedy, where the fall after the peak is the point.

The hero's journey suits myth and adventure, the departure from the ordinary world, the trials, the ordeal, the return transformed.

Kishotenketsu, a four-part structure from East Asian tradition, builds on contrast rather than conflict: introduction, development, twist, reconciliation. It suits quieter, literary work where the engine isn't a fight but a shift in understanding.

Nonlinear, braided, and fragmented structures exist for specific effects, telling events out of order, running multiple timelines, breaking the narrative into pieces that assemble in the reader's mind.

## Choose with intent

The question isn't which structure is best. It's which one serves your story. A revenge tragedy wants Freytag. A coming-of-age adventure wants the hero's journey. A meditative literary novel might want kishotenketsu, where forcing conflict-based three-act would flatten it. Pick deliberately. And if three-act still fits best, good, use it knowing you chose it rather than defaulted to it.
