# Week 2 — The Premise

Why the idea isn't enough: a good idea feels like a book but usually isn't one. Most ideas are a premise for a scene, not an engine for 300 pages. The test: can this idea generate conflict and pressure for a whole book, or does it burn out in twenty pages? Ideas are cheap. The skill is spotting the one with enough pressure to sustain a novel.

Where the pressure comes from: durable ideas come from collision. Not "a detective solves a murder," but a character in a situation that threatens what they most need. The engine isn't the event, it's what the event costs the character.

The premise line: compress the whole novel into one sentence. When [inciting event] happens, [protagonist] must [goal] or [stakes], but [obstacle]. It's a diagnostic, not marketing. If you can't fill a blank, that's a hole in the book. Weak obstacle means a saggy middle. Vague stakes means nobody cares. Refine until the obstacle genuinely worries you.

Two questions that harden it: "What if?" gives the hook. "Why now?" gives the entry point. The story has to start the day the character's normal life becomes impossible to keep. If it could start a year earlier or later, you haven't found the right opening.

Task: Draft your premise line and pressure-test it until the obstacle worries you. Fill every blank, then attack the weakest part and strengthen it until the whole sentence has weight.
