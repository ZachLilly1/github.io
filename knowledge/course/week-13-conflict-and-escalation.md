# Week 13 — Conflict and Escalation

## Conflict is the fuel

Conflict is what drives narrative, and it runs on three levels at once. Internal, the character against themselves. Interpersonal, character against character. External, character against society, nature, or fate. The richest books run all three at the same time. Every scene needs at least one active conflict. When a scene goes slack, the diagnosis is almost always the same: nobody wants anything, or the wanting isn't opposed.

## Escalation is the climb

Conflict must intensify. This is where amateur plots die, they plateau. Each obstacle should be harder than the last. Each setback should cost more. Professional plots climb, and the tool for climbing is the try-fail cycle.

The character attempts a solution and fails in a way that makes the situation worse, then must try again against steeper odds. Try, fail, try harder, fail worse. Each failure raises the stakes and narrows the options. The reader feels the walls closing in because they actually are. If your Act Two sags, it's almost always because the stakes stopped rising. You're generating new events but not new pressure.

## New events versus new pressure

This is the distinction to burn in. A plot can have a lot happening and still feel flat, because motion isn't the same as escalation. Ask of every scene: is the pressure higher than it was in the last scene? If things merely happen without the vise tightening, the reader disengages even amid action. Rising pressure, not rising activity, is what holds them.

## Task

Build your try-and-fail cycles through the middle of the book so tension climbs instead of leveling off. For each major attempt, make the failure cost more than the last and narrow the character's options. Check that every stretch raises the pressure, not just the activity.
