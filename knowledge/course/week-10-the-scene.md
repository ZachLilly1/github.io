# Week 10 — The Scene

## The unit everything is built from

A novel is built from scenes, and every scene has to earn its place. This is the week you learn the unit, because a book is only as strong as its weakest scene, and most saggy novels sag at the scene level.

## Goal, conflict, turn

A functioning scene has three things. A point-of-view character enters with a goal, something they want in this scene. An obstacle or conflict stands in the way. And the scene turns, it ends in a different state than it began, usually with the character worse off or facing a new complication. A scene where nothing changes is a scene to cut. If your character enters and leaves in the same emotional and situational place, you wrote a stretch of nothing.

## Scene and sequel

The classic rhythm is scene, then sequel. The scene is action: goal, conflict, disaster. The sequel is reaction: the character absorbs what happened, faces a dilemma, and makes a decision that launches the next goal. The sequel is where interiority lives, where the character processes and chooses, and it's what makes the next scene feel caused rather than random. Not every beat needs a full sequel, but the rhythm of push and process is what gives a book its pulse.

## Cause and effect

Scenes connect by causation, not sequence. "The king died and then the queen died" is sequence. "The king died and then the queen died of grief" is plot. Each scene should be the consequence of the last and the cause of the next. Test it: if you can join two scenes with "and then," your plot is a list. If you can join them with "therefore" or "but," your plot is working. Hunt down every "and then" and turn it into a "therefore."

## Task

Break your first act into scenes. Give each one a POV character, a goal, a conflict, and a turn. Then check the connections. Every scene should link to the next by "therefore" or "but," never "and then."
